Policy Gradient Loss (log-likelihood intuition)

A common Monte-Carlo / one-step form of the policy-gradient loss at time step t is:

\(\text{loss}_t = -\log\big(P(a_t \mid s_t)\big)\,R_t\)

Core idea (what the loss is doing)

For the sampled pair \((s_t, a_t)\), your policy assigns a probability to the action that you actually took. Taking the log gives a log-likelihood; negating it gives the standard negative log-likelihood form. 

loss_t = - log( P(a_t | s_t) ) * R_t

During training you typically minimize this loss with gradient descent. Intuition: if R_t > 0, minimizing the loss increases P(a_t | s_t); if R_t < 0, it decreases P(a_t | s_t).

What the symbols mean

  • s_t: the state at time step t
  • a_t: the action actually sampled/taken at time step t
  • P(a_t | s_t) (often written π_θ(a_t | s_t)): the policy’s probability of choosing that particular action in that state
  • R_t: the return signal associated with time t (often discounted sum of future rewards)

Why this form shows up (the log-gradient connection)

The policy-gradient theorem gives a gradient of expected return that (schematically) looks like: 

∇_θ J(θ) = E[ Σ_t  ∇_θ log( π_θ(a_t | s_t) ) * R_t ]

The key trick is the “log trick”: ∇_θ log(π_θ(a_t|s_t)) tells you how changing parameters would change the log-probability of the action you sampled. Multiplying by R_t weights that update by “how good that time was”. Using a loss of the form -log(π_θ(a_t|s_t)) * R_t makes gradient descent move toward higher return.

“Log-likelihood of the action” (meaning in this context)

The policy π(a|s) = P(a|s) is a probability distribution over actions. For the specific sampled pair \((s_t, a_t)\), the policy assigns a probability \(P(a_t \mid s_t)\) to the action that actually occurred.

\(\log(P(a_t \mid s_t))\)

That quantity is the log-likelihood of the action under the policy. The loss uses its negative, so minimizing the loss corresponds to maximizing log-probability of good actions and decreasing log-probability of bad actions (as indicated by R_t or an advantage).

Why the subscript t appears everywhere

You do this computation at every time step along a trajectory. Each step yields its own s_t, sampled a_t, and its own return target R_t. The final update is based on the sum (or average) over all time steps.

Note: Many lectures replace R_t with an advantage A_t (baseline-subtracted, variance-reduced return). The same intuition applies: positive advantage pushes the probability of the action up; negative advantage pushes it down.
